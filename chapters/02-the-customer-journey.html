The customer's journey

Last chapter ended with a mystery: 260 people tapped Meena's ad, but only 31 ordered. It's tempting to call the other 229 "lost." Most of them weren't. They just weren't ready. This chapter is about the road between "never heard of you" and "telling their friends about you."

One of the 229

Tuesday, 2 pm. Priya is at her desk, scrolling. Meena's ghee-roast ad appears. It looks great; she taps it, stares at the photo… and closes it. She's already had lunch. She is now one of the 229.

Thursday. A colleague mentions "that new dosa place with the crazy ghee roast." Priya thinks: oh, that one.

Friday. The ad appears again. This time she searches the shop on Google. 4.6 stars, lots of reviews, photos that match the ad. Okay. Seems legit.

Sunday, 9 am. Lazy morning, no plans for breakfast. She remembers, taps "Order on WhatsApp," pays. Her first order.

Two weeks later. A WhatsApp message from the shop: "Sunday special: ghee roast + filter coffee combo." She orders again.

That evening. She posts a photo on her Instagram story, tagging the shop. Three friends ask where it's from.

🔎 Pause and notice Count the touches before Priya paid: an ad, a tap, a colleague, a second ad, Google reviews. Five, spread over six days. If Meena had judged Tuesday's ad only by Tuesday's orders, what would she have wrongly concluded?

Why don't people buy right away?

Go back to basics. Every purchase is a small decision made with incomplete information. A person only acts when what they gain clearly outweighs what they risk. You can write that as a rough equation:

They act when
Desire × Trust  >  Price + Effort + Risk

Now replay Priya's week through this equation:

  • Tuesday: desire was low (she'd eaten) and trust was zero (never heard of the shop). Equation fails. No order.
  • Thursday–Friday: the colleague and the reviews pushed trust up.
  • Sunday: hungry, no plans, so desire was up, and one-tap WhatsApp kept effort low. Equation passes. She orders.
💡 The core idea A customer's journey is just this equation being pushed, one term at a time, until it tips. Each stage of the journey exists to move one of those terms. Marketing's job is to know which term is blocking someone right now.

The 5 stages

Marketers name the stages so they can measure and fix each one separately. Each stage is a question in the customer's head. Tap a stage to open it.

🔄 It's a loop, not a ladder Look at where Priya's story ended: her Instagram story put three friends into Awareness. Stage 5 feeds stage 1. A business whose customers keep talking about it doesn't have to keep paying for every new one. Remember Chapter 1's feedback loop? This is the same shape, at the level of people.

Match the message to the stage

Here's the most common beginner mistake: showing a stranger an ad that screams "ORDER NOW, 20% OFF!"

That's proposing marriage on a first meeting. The person has no trust yet, so the pushier you are, the faster they scroll past. Each stage needs a different kind of message:

Wrong stage

To a stranger

"Order now! Limited offer!"

Right stage

To a stranger

A mouth-watering 7-second reel of the ghee roast being folded.

Wrong stage

To a regular

"Hi! We're a new dosa shop near you!"

Right stage

To a regular

"Your usual this Sunday? Combo's on."

🧠 Compress it Before writing any message, ask: "Which stage is this person in, and which term of the equation is blocking them?" Then write only to that blocker.

Quiz: pull it out of memory

No scrolling up. Wrong answers are welcome; the explanation is where the learning happens.

1. Priya tapped the ad on Tuesday but didn't order. What's the most accurate description of her?
She moved into awareness and touched consideration. Five days later she ordered. Judging Tuesday's ad only by Tuesday's orders would have hidden that.
2. In the equation Desire × Trust > Price + Effort + Risk, what do Google reviews mainly increase?
Reviews are evidence from strangers. They don't make you hungrier; they make you believe the shop is good. That's trust, the main job of the consideration stage.
3. A person who has never heard of your brand sees "BUY NOW, 20% OFF!" Why does this usually fail?
A stranger is in awareness. Their blocker is "I don't know you," not price. A discount solves a problem they don't have yet.
4. Plenty of people order once, but very few order a second time. Which stage needs the most work?
They trusted you enough to buy once. The leak is after the first order. That's retention: reminders, reasons to return, a good first experience.
5. Why is the customer journey better drawn as a loop than a straight line?
Priya's Instagram story pushed three friends into awareness for free. Advocacy feeds awareness, so a good business grows partly on its own.

🛠️ Your turn: map your own journey

Think of the last thing you bought online that cost more than a meal: a gadget, a course, shoes, anything.

  1. List every touch you can remember before buying: ads, searches, reviews, a friend's comment, a YouTube video.
  2. Label each one with its stage: aware, consider, convert.
  3. Find the tipping point. Which touch finally made the equation pass? Which term did it move: desire, trust, or effort?
  4. Since then: has the brand done anything to retain you? Have you recommended it to anyone?

Most people are surprised how many touches it took. You're doing what marketers call journey mapping, using the one customer you know best: yourself.
