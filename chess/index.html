THE FIRST PRINCIPLE

How do you rob a museum?

Picture a museum at night. The diamond sits in a glass case. What actually protects it?

Not "the museum". Three specific things: a guard standing next to it, a line of sight from the camera in the corner, and time, because the alarm gives the police a few minutes to arrive. Take away any one of those three and the diamond is yours.

A chess defence is built from exactly the same three materials. Every square near the king and every piece worth protecting is defended by someone (a guard), usually along a line (rooks, bishops and queens defend from far away), and only if the defender has time to respond. So there are exactly three ways to break in:

The question that finds tactics: before looking for clever moves, point at each enemy defender and ask, "What is your job? What are you standing on? How much time do you have?" The weak answer is where the combination is.

Every combination on this page uses one of these seven patterns, and the great ones stack two or three. Each pattern below starts with a famous game you can step through, move by move.

Pattern 01 · attack the guard

Deflection

Pull the guard away from the gate.

Back to the museum. The guard by the diamond is huge, armed and loyal. You can't beat him. So you set off the fire alarm in the other wing. He runs. He's still huge, armed and loyal. He's just not there any more.

That's deflection. You don't defeat the defender. You give it an order it can't refuse (a check to answer, a piece to recapture, a threat to stop) and that order takes it somewhere else.

First principle

A defender is not a piece. It's a piece on a square. Its defensive power exists only from where it stands. Change the square, and the defence disappears even though the piece is still alive.

A mating square or promotion square guarded by one piece.
You have a check or capture that the guard is the only piece able to answer.
The guard is a blocker: it sits on a line you need open.
Deflection vs. just capturing the defender: what's the real difference?
tap to reveal
Capturing the guard ("removing the defender") kills it. Deflection keeps it alive but relocates it. Both leave the job undone. Deflection is often the only option when you can't capture the guard, and it usually costs you the bait.
Pattern 02 · attack the guard

Decoy

Invite the king to a dinner he can't leave.

A mousetrap doesn't chase the mouse. It decides where the mouse will be, then makes that spot irresistible.

Some squares on the board are "loaded": stand there and you're on the same line as an enemy rook, or on the fork square of a knight, or in a corner where your own pieces will smother you. A decoy sacrifice puts the bait on that square. The enemy has to take it, and taking it means standing on the trap.

First principle

Deflection cares where the piece leaves. Decoy cares where it arrives. If you can name a square where the enemy king (or queen) would be lost, ask: what can I sacrifice on that square that he must take?

A square next to the king that's on one of your lines (rook file, bishop diagonal).
A knight-fork square, or a square that allows a double check.
A sacrifice with only one legal way to capture it.
Why are double checks the decoy's best friend?
tap to reveal
Against a double check you can't block and you can't capture both checkers. The only legal answer is a king move. So once the king has been lured into a double check, you choose where he walks, and you've already decided where that ends.
Pattern 03 · attack the guard

Overloading

One guard, two gates.

A waiter serving two tables does fine until both tables raise their hands at the same moment. He hasn't become a worse waiter. He has just been asked to be in two places at once.

In chess, defenders quietly collect jobs: guard the back rank, protect that knight, cover that square. Each job alone is easy. An overloaded defender is one whose jobs can be called in at the same time.

First principle

Defence is accounting. List the important targets and write the defenders next to each one. If the same defender's name appears twice, that defender is overloaded: hit one target and the other is free. Often you don't even need a sacrifice, just a capture.

A piece that is the only guard of two different things.
A back rank whose only guard is also defending something else.
Pawns sheltering the king that also guard squares in front of them.
Is the queen too strong to be overloaded?
tap to reveal
No. The queen is the most overloaded piece in most games, precisely because she's strong enough to be given every job. Power doesn't protect a defender. The number of jobs it has does. Watch for her in the case files.
Pattern 04 · attack the line

Interference

Build a wall across the line of sight.

The museum camera in the corner watches the diamond from forty metres away. You don't need to smash the camera. You just need to wheel a tall statue in front of it.

Rooks, bishops and queens defend from a distance, along a line. A line has empty squares on it, and every empty square is a place where something can be put. Interference drops a piece onto the line, and suddenly the far-away guard sees nothing.

First principle

A long-range defence is only as strong as the emptiest square on its line. Interference works when capturing the intruder costs too much, or, deadliest of all, when the intruder stands where two defensive lines cross, so whichever defender captures it blocks the other.

A rook, bishop or queen guarding something from far away.
An empty square on that line that you can reach, ideally with a protected piece.
Two enemy defensive lines that cross on one square.
Why can't you interfere with a knight's defence?
tap to reveal
A knight jumps. It has no line, so there's nothing to block. Interference only works against the "line pieces": bishops, rooks and queens. Against a knight-guard you have to go back to the guard family: deflect it, decoy it, or overload it.
Pattern 05 · attack the line

Clearance

Your own piece is the traffic jam.

An ambulance is stuck behind a car. The car isn't an enemy. It's just in the way. The fix: the car pulls over, fast, before the gap closes again.

Attacks need open lines and empty squares. Very often the piece clogging your attack is your own: a knight in front of your queen, a rook on the square your knight needs. Clearance gets it out of the way, and the art is to make it leave with a threat, so the opponent has no time to plug the hole.

First principle

Interference blocks their line. Clearance opens yours. A clearing move must cost the opponent a tempo (check, capture, or a threat he must answer), or he will simply close the line again.

A battery (queen + bishop, rook + rook) where the front piece is yours and blocking.
A key square occupied by your own piece.
A sacrifice that seems to do nothing except vacate a square.
Go back to Fischer's 19.Rf6 in the Interference game. Interference or clearance?
tap to reveal
Both. Rf6 cut the g7-bishop's line to e5 (interference), so that e4-e5 could then open the d3-bishop's diagonal to h7 (clearance). The best combinations stack patterns, which is why you learn them separately first.
Pattern 06 · attack the clock

Zwischenzug (tsvish-en-tsook)

The expected reply can wait.

Someone insults you. Everyone in the room waits for your comeback. Instead you say, calmly: "Your car is being towed." They run outside. You can deliver the comeback later, if you still need to.

Zwischenzug is German for "in-between move". Chess feels like a conversation (he takes, I take back), and that feeling is a trap. Before the obvious reply, you can insert a move that hurts more: a check, a bigger threat, a capture elsewhere. The original question doesn't go away. It just gets answered on your terms.

First principle

A move should be judged by its consequences, not by what it's "replying to". If the enemy piece that just captured is stuck (it can't escape), your recapture will still be there in one move, so spend this move on something bigger first.

A piece just captured and it's trapped or can't retreat.
You have a check available before recapturing.
Both sides have pieces hanging. Whoever moves decides the order.
What's the 3-second habit that finds zwischenzugs?
tap to reveal
Before every "obvious" move, especially a recapture, scan for checks, captures, threats, in that order, for both sides. Strong players don't have better instincts here. They just refuse to skip the scan.
Pattern 07 · attack the clock

The Windmill

The sails never stop turning.

A revolving door: every push brings the next panel around. You never get a gap to slip through the other way.

A discovered check is the strongest move in chess: the front piece moves wherever it likes, and the piece behind it gives check. Now imagine the front piece can come back with a check of its own, and then leave again with another discovered check. The defender spends every move answering checks while your piece eats everything along its path.

First principle

Time is a resource, and each check costs the defender one move. A discovered check gives you a free move on top of the check. A windmill chains them in a loop (direct check, discovered check, direct check...) so the defender never gets a single free move.

Your piece stands in front of your rook/bishop/queen, aimed at the enemy king.
The enemy king is boxed in, with only two squares to shuttle between.
Loose enemy pieces along the path your front piece can travel.
Why must the windmill alternate a direct check with a discovered check?
tap to reveal
The front piece has to get back to its loaded square (g7 in Torre's game) without giving the opponent a free move, so it returns with check. Then it leaves again with a discovered check. If even one move in the loop is quiet, the defender gets the move he needs to break the line.
