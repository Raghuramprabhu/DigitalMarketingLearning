Build the same house

The internet loves to mock it as "the boring opening". World champions keep winning with it. Magnus Carlsen made it his trademark, and in 2023 Ding Liren used it to win a game of the World Championship match.

The London isn't a list of moves to memorise. It's a house plan: one setup, a few rules, and a handful of plans that work against almost anything Black does. Learn the plan and you can play it on move 5 or move 25.

THE FIRST PRINCIPLE

Why a system beats a memory test

Most openings are like learning a city by memorising every street. Take one wrong turn on move 7 and you're lost. The London works the other way round: you carry your own house with you and build it wherever you land.

d4 · Bf4 · e3 · Nf3 · c3 · Nbd2 · Bd3 · (h3) · castle

A bit of history first. In the 1880s an Irish-American master, James Mason, kept playing an early Bf4 that others called an "irregular opening". It got its name when it showed up seven times at the great London 1922 tournament, including games by Capablanca, Alekhine and Rubinstein. A century later it's one of the most popular openings in club chess.

Idea 01 · build the house

Bishop out first

Get the bike out of the garage before you park the car in front of it.

Every 1.d4 player has the same headache: the bishop on c1. Play e3 too early and it's walled in behind its own pawns, like a bike stuck behind the car in the garage. The older Colle system lives with that. The London fixes it with one simple rule.

Bf4 comes before e3. Bishop out, then close the door. Everything else in the setup is flexible.

First principle

Your pawns will end up on c3, d4 and e3, which are dark squares. The bishop that moves on dark squares would be blocked by them, so it must get outside the chain first. The light-squared bishop goes to d3, where the pawns don't block it.

Is the c1 bishop already outside before you play e3?
Knight to d2, not c3: c3 is reserved for the pawn.
Bd3 aims at h7, the target of half the London's attacks.
Why is the knight developed to d2 instead of the more natural c3?
tap to reveal
Because c3 belongs to the pawn. The c3 pawn completes the pyramid that makes d4 unbreakable, and it gives the queen the b3 and c2 squares. From d2 the knight still supports e4 and can go to f3 or b3.
Idea 02 · build the house

The pyramid and the knight on e5

The house comes with a guest room. The knight lives there.

When Black sets up with ...d5 and ...e6, something quietly happens: neither black pawn can ever attack e5 again. Your d4 pawn guards it. It's an outpost handed to you by the structure, a guest room in the enemy's half.

Put a knight there and support it with f2-f4, and you get the Stonewall formation: a knight on e5 that nothing can shift, and a straight path for your queen and rook toward Black's king.

First principle

A pawn chain defends and creates squares. The pyramid c3-d4-e3 hands you e5. If Black trades your knight there, recapture with a pawn (dxe5 or fxe5) and you gain space, often kicking his f6 knight away from his king.

Black has ...d5 and ...e6: e5 is yours.
No black knight or bishop can easily trade on e5: go there.
Plan after Ne5 + f4: Qf3, Rf3-h3, or g4-g5.
Black can't kick your e5 knight with a pawn. So what can he do?
tap to reveal
He can trade it (...Nxe5, ...Bxe5, or ...Nd7 to challenge it), or play ...f6, which weakens e6 and his king. When he trades, recapture with a pawn: a white pawn on e5 kicks the f6 knight away and cramps Black's kingside.
Idea 03 · guard the house

The b2 question

Every house has a back door. Know where yours is.

When the bishop leaves c1 it takes away the only minor piece that guarded b2. Black's most popular plan against the London (...c5 plus ...Qb6) knocks on exactly that door, hitting b2 and d4 at once.

Two ways to answer. The calm way: guard it with Qb3 (offering a queen trade), Qc1 or Qc2. The cheeky way: leave it and make the pawn poisonous. When Black's queen goes to b2 she's far from home, and the square c7 becomes a fork square for your knight.

First principle

A grabbed pawn costs the grabber time and distance: a queen on b2 or a2 can be chased (Rb1) and is far from her king. But punishing the grab needs a target. Look for c7: if it's unguarded, Nb5 or Nxd5 threatens Nc7+, forking king and rook.

...c5 and ...Qb6 on the board: decide now. Guard (Qb3/Qc1) or gambit (Nc3)?
Black's queen on b2: Rb1 hits it with tempo.
Is c7 guarded? If not, a knight on b5 or d5 forks.
When does the knight-to-c7 trick NOT work?
tap to reveal
When c7 is guarded. Stockfish shows it clearly: after 1.d4 Nf6 2.Bf4 c5 3.e3 Qb6 4.Nc3 Qxb2, Black has no pawn on d5 yet, so ...Nd5 can come back to guard c7 and the trick only equalises. With the pawn on d5, the knight can't get to d5, and the trick wins. Same idea, different board: always check the fork square.
Idea 04 · guard the house

Keep the bishop, or take the h-file

If someone takes your bicycle, at least make them leave the gate open.

The f4-bishop is the London's main character, so Black spends a lot of energy trying to remove it: ...Bd6 offering a trade, or ...Nh5 chasing it. You don't have to panic. Step back to g3 (or h2, after h3) and keep it on the same diagonal.

And if Black does take it on g3? Recapture hxg3. Your pawns look ugly, but the h-file is now open, your rook on h1 points straight at h7, and Black's castled king has a new problem.

First principle

Every trade changes the structure, so ask what the recapture gives you. hxg3 trades a bishop for an open file aimed at the enemy king. That's why London players often delay castling short: the rook on h1 might be more useful staying where it is.

...Bd6: answer Bg3, keeping the diagonal.
...Nh5: the bishop needs a safe square (g3 or h2). An early h3 prepares h2.
...Bxg3: recapture hxg3 and look at h7.
After ...Bxg3, why recapture with the h-pawn and not the f-pawn?
tap to reveal
hxg3 opens the h-file toward Black's king and keeps your f-pawn free for f4 or e3-e4 ideas. fxg3 opens the f-file but leaves e3 a lonely, backward pawn. The doubled g-pawns after hxg3 sound bad, but they control f4 and h4 and can even advance (Carlsen's g3-g4 in the famous games below).
Idea 05 · guard the house

Against the fianchetto (King's Indian setup)

Before you answer a knock at the door, count who's outside.

Some opponents don't play ...d5 at all. They put a bishop on g7, a pawn on d6, and wait to hit you with ...e5, gaining the centre and attacking your bishop at the same time.

The London's answer is a few quiet, wise moves: h3 to give the bishop a safe square on h2, a modest Be2, and castling. Then when ...e5 comes, you count before you react.

First principle

A pawn break is a counting problem. Your f4-bishop and f3-knight both hit e5. If Black plays ...e5 with fewer defenders than you have attackers, dxe5 wins a pawn. If he has prepared it properly (...Nbd7, ...Re8, ...Qe7), retreat with Bh2 and keep the pressure.

...g6 and ...Bg7: play h3 early, so the bishop has h2.
Be2, not Bd3: on d3 the bishop gets hit by ...e5-e4.
...e5 on the board: count attackers and defenders before retreating.
Why is the "book move" Bh2 sometimes a mistake after ...e5?
tap to reveal
Because books describe the prepared ...e5. When Black pushes it early, with e5 attacked twice and defended once, Stockfish prefers dxe5, winning a pawn by simple counting. Books give you the plan; counting tells you if the plan applies right now. The Arena has an example.
Idea 06 · strike from the house

The h7 battery

The guard on h7 has one friend. Watch the friend.

Your bishop on d3 has stared at h7 since move 8. Your queen can join it from c2 or h5, and after hxg3 your rook stares at it from h1. In most games nothing happens, because Black's knight on f6 guards h7.

But knights like to move. The instant it leaves f6 (to d7, e4, or anywhere), h7 might be left with no defender at all, and the most famous sacrifice in chess appears: Bxh7+, the Greek gift.

First principle

A battery is two or more pieces on one line. h7 is defended only by the king and the f6-knight, so when that knight moves, count the battery. Bishop plus queen (or plus the h-file rook) against the king alone usually means a sacrifice is worth calculating.

The black knight just left f6.
Your queen can reach h5 or the h-file fast, or your rook is already on h1.
A white knight can jump to g5 or e5 to join the attack.
Name three conditions that make Bxh7+ work.
tap to reveal
(1) No knight on f6 guarding h7. (2) Fast reinforcements: a knight to g5 or e5, and a queen to h5, or a rook already on the h-file. (3) Black can't easily cover g5 or the h-file (no dark-squared bishop on e7, say). Missing one condition? Calculate carefully. Missing two? Don't sacrifice.
Idea 07 · strike from the house

Against the mirror (...Bf5)

When someone copies your homework, ask what they skipped.

The London's trick is getting the bishop outside the pawn chain. So a clever opponent just copies it: 2...Bf5, and now his problem bishop is free too. It's the most annoying reply there is.

You can't out-copy a copier, so you change the subject. Either trade his copy with Bd3, or attack what his bishop left behind: with the bishop gone from c8, the pawn on b7 has lost a guard, and c4 plus Qb3 hits it.

First principle

Every move leaves something behind. When a piece leaves home, the squares and pawns it used to protect become a little weaker. Against the mirror, the weak spots are b7 and the long-term light squares.

Black's bishop is on f5: offer the trade with Bd3.
Bishop gone from c8: is b7 hanging after c4 and Qb3?
Locked centre after ...c4: play a4-a5 on the queenside (Carlsen vs Giri).
Black played ...Bf5, ...e6 and ...Nd7, but not ...Qb6 or ...Qc8. You've played c4 and Qb3. What do you check first?
tap to reveal
b7. The bishop that guarded it is on f5, the knight on d7 doesn't guard it, and the queen on d8 doesn't either. If Qxb7 doesn't walk into a trap, it's a free pawn. There's an Arena puzzle on exactly this.
MIDDLEGAME RADAR

"When you see this, think that."

The London's middlegames repeat themselves, which is its biggest gift to a hobby player. Keep these ideas on your radar during a game.
