All three patterns below are tricks for turning the red number into the green one. The secret ingredient is always the same: some order in the data tells you which way to move, so you never waste a step.

Your turn: the fence postsMedium · guess first, then reveal

Posts of different heights stand in a row, 1 metre apart. Stretch a tarpaulin between any two to catch rain. Water only rises as high as the shorter of the two, and a wider gap holds more.

Water = shorter post's height × distance between them. The posts are not sorted, and you can't rearrange them.

Q1. The fingers start on post #1 (1m) and post #9 (7m). Which finger should step inward first?
The shorter post sets the water level. Moving the taller one can only shrink the width while the short post still caps the level, so it can only get worse. Moving the short one is the only move that might find a taller post.
Q2. Why is it safe to throw the shorter post away for good?
This is the bit that felt slippery on the walk. You never search for the shortest post in the whole row; you only compare the two posts under your fingers. The shorter one has just had its best possible chance, so nothing is lost by dropping it.
Q3. Which pair holds the most water?
#2 (8m) and #9 (7m): level 7 × width 7 = 49. The two tallest (#2 & #7) only manage 8 × 5 = 40, and the widest pair is stuck at 1 × 8 = 8. The winner balances height and width. Run the playground below to watch the two fingers find it in 8 steps.

Sliding windowA frame of fixed size, gliding along

🚆

From a train window you see exactly three trees. As the train moves, one tree slides in on the right and one slides out on the left. The trees in the middle don't change, so you never re-count them.

The lazy way

+++

Re-add all k items for every window: a loop inside a loop.

The window way

−+

Carry the total forward: add the newcomer, subtract the leaver. One pass.

The clue in a problem: the stretch is consecutive and its size is given (3 hours, 7 days, a 3-letter word).

The window doesn't have to hold a sum. Here it holds a set of the last 2 songs, and the question is whether the new song is already in it.

Count every 3-day stretch whose average was at least 5 km. One loop does both jobs: slide, then judge.

Mediumfixed window · tallies

Find TEA, shuffled

Comparing words letter by letter at every spot would be slow. Instead the window keeps a tally of its letters; a shuffle of TEA is any window whose tally is exactly A:1 E:1 T:1.

The caterpillarTwo pointers + a window: the hybrid

🐛

Now let the window breathe. A caterpillar's head (right) keeps reaching forward. When a rule breaks, its tail (left) catches up until the rule heals. Two pointers, both walking the same way, with the window between them as the answer.

The clue: the stretch is consecutive, but its size is what you're hunting ("longest…", "shortest…", "how many stretches…").

The caterpillar's one conditionShrinking from the back must always help the rule heal. With costs that are never negative, dropping a day always lowers the total. If some numbers are negative, that promise breaks, and so does the caterpillar.

Why is it still one pass when there's a while inside the for? Because neither end ever moves backwards. Each item enters the window once and leaves at most once.

Spotting guideAsk these before you code

👉👈

Two pointers if you're working with two items at a time (pairs, swaps, copies), usually from both ends of a sorted or symmetric row, or with a slow finger writing while a fast one reads. What lies between the fingers isn't the answer.

🪟

Sliding window if the answer is a consecutive stretch of a size you're told. Add one in, drop one out.

🐛

Caterpillar if the answer is a consecutive stretch whose size you're hunting, and shrinking from the back always heals the rule.

🤔

Something else if order doesn't matter, the data isn't a row, or none of the above quite fits. Don't force it. Not every problem is a nail.
