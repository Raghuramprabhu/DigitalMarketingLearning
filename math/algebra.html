ACT I LEVEL 1

The Balance

How do you find a number that nobody will tell you?

c. 820 CE · BAGHDAD

A library that swallowed the world

Under Caliph al-Ma'mūn, Baghdad's House of Wisdom is collecting knowledge from everywhere: Greek geometry, Persian astronomy, and from India, a strange new way of writing numbers using just ten symbols and a zero.

A Persian scholar there, Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī, writes a short, practical handbook. Not for mathematicians. For people with real problems: how do I split this inheritance fairly? how much land is this? how do I price this trade?

The book is called al-Kitāb al-mukhtaṣar fī ḥisāb al-jabr wa-l-muqābala: "The Compact Book on Calculating by Restoring and Balancing."

THE TWO MOVES

No x. No =. Just words.

There are no symbols anywhere in the book. Every problem is a sentence. One of them: "A square and ten roots equal thirty-nine dirhams." (Hold on to that sentence; it comes back in Act IV.)

To solve them, al-Khwārizmī uses just two moves:

al-jabr, restoring: if something has been taken away on one side, add it back to both sides, so nothing is left "broken".
al-muqābala, balancing: if the same thing sits on both sides, remove it from both.

That's it. Those two moves are still how every equation in the world gets solved.

1145 → 1557 · EUROPE

A name becomes two words; two lines become a sign

Robert of Chester translates the book into Latin; al-jabr becomes algebra. The author's own name, Latinised as Algoritmi, becomes algorithm.

For four more centuries, equations are still written in words. Then in 1557 the Welsh doctor Robert Recorde, tired of writing "is equal to" over and over, invents =: a pair of parallel lines of the same length, because, he explained, no two things could be more equal.

Why did "algebra" once mean setting broken bones?
tap to reveal
Al-jabr literally means reuniting broken parts. In medieval Spain an algebrista was a bone-setter, and in Don Quixote a battered character is treated by one. It's the perfect metaphor: when you solve an equation, you're "resetting" a quantity that has been broken off one side and restoring it to its rightful place.
🧱
First principles

What is an equation? The = sign is a promise: "these two piles weigh exactly the same." If you do the same thing to both piles, the promise stays true. That is the one rule of algebra. Everything else is clever ways of using it.

🧠 Recall before you scroll on
What does al-jabr literally mean?
Restoring / reuniting broken parts (even bone-setting). In equations: add back what was taken away, on both sides.
What is the ONE rule of solving equations?
"=" is a promise of balance. Whatever you do to one side, do to the other.
Who invented "=" and why two lines?
Robert Recorde, 1557. Nothing is more equal than two parallel lines of the same length.

One unknown is one number hiding in a box. But real questions are rarely "what is x?" They're "how does my bill change as the kilometres climb?" For that, a French philosopher had to turn algebra into a picture.

ACT II LEVEL 2

The Line

What if you could see an equation, and every point on the picture were an answer?

1637 · LEIDEN

Descartes marries algebra and geometry

René Descartes publishes La Géométrie, an appendix to his famous Discourse on Method. Two small decisions in it shaped every maths class since.

One: letters near the end of the alphabet (x, y, z) stand for unknowns; letters near the start (a, b, c) for known numbers.

Two: any point on a flat surface can be named by two numbers: how far across, how far up. So an equation with x and y isn't just a sentence anymore. It's a shape.

LEGEND The story goes that he got the idea lying in bed, watching a fly crawl on the ceiling and realising its position could be described by its distance from two walls. Probably invented, but the idea is real. In France, Pierre de Fermat reached the same idea independently around the same time.

So why is the unknown called "x", of all letters?
tap to reveal
A popular story says Arabic shay' ("thing") became Spanish xei, shortened to x. It's charming, but historians have found little evidence for it. Another tale says Descartes's printer had spare x's. Also unproven. The honest answer is boring: Descartes used the last letters for unknowns, and x simply won the popularity contest. Sometimes the truth is less interesting than the myth, and a good thinker notices which is which.
📏
First principles

Anything that changes at a steady rate draws a straight line: y = m·x + c c is what you pay just for showing up (the boarding fee). m is how much more each extra step costs (₹ per km). Taxi fares, phone plans, salary plus overtime, the water bill: all lines.

Playground · the break-even

Cab, or rent a scooter for the day?

Cab: ₹50 base + ₹18 per km. Scooter rental: ₹250 for the day + about ₹3 per km in fuel. The cab is cheaper for short hops. The scooter wins on long days. Where exactly does the switch happen?

🎯 Gut feeling first: the scooter starts winning after about km
Celsius and Fahrenheit are both lines. Is there a temperature where they show the same number?
tap to reveal
Set them equal: C = 1.8C + 32 → −0.8C = 32 → C = −40 At −40°, both thermometers agree. Two lines, one crossing point: the same idea as the cab and the scooter.
💳

Real decision: a credit card with a ₹500 yearly fee gives 1.5% cashback. A free card gives 0.5%. Which is better? Let s = what you spend in a year. Break-even: 0.015s − 500 = 0.005s → 0.01s = 500 → s = ₹50,000 Spend more than ₹50,000 a year and the fee card pays for itself. Less, and you're paying for the privilege. Same reasoning settles buy-vs-rent, monthly gym vs pay-per-visit, prepaid vs postpaid.

🧠 Recall before you scroll on
In y = mx + c, what do m and c mean in real life?
m = the rate (₹ per km, the slope). c = the starting amount at x = 0 (the base fee).
What does the crossing point of two lines tell you?
The x where both options are equal: the break-even. Left of it one wins, right of it the other.
Where do Celsius and Fahrenheit agree?
At −40°. Found by setting the two lines equal.

That crossing point answered two things at once: the kilometres and the cost. Two unknowns, cracked together. Five centuries before Descartes, an astronomer in Ujjain was setting puzzles like this in Sanskrit verse, for his daughter, or so the legend goes.

ACT III LEVEL 3

Two Unknowns

Two mysteries. Two clues. Neither clue alone is enough. Together, they crack both.

c. 200 BCE – 200 CE · CHINA

Counting rods on a table

The Chinese classic Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art solves problems like "3 bundles of good grain and 2 of poor yield 39 dou…" by laying out counting rods in a grid and cancelling one unknown at a time. It's essentially the method of elimination you'll use below, written down around two thousand years before Europe gave it Gauss's name.

1150 · UJJAIN

Bhāskara writes maths as poetry

Bhāskara II (Bhāskarāchārya), head of the observatory at Ujjain, writes Līlāvatī, a book of arithmetic and algebra in Sanskrit verse. Its problems are addressed to a young girl, and they're full of bees, lotuses, peacocks, pearls and merchants.

LEGEND Astrologers fixed one auspicious moment for Līlāvatī's wedding, timed by a cup with a hole sinking slowly in water. A pearl fell from her jewellery, blocked the hole, and the moment silently passed. To console her, her father named his book after her. Lovely, unprovable, and it made sure nobody forgot the book.

Bhāskara's bee riddle: one-fifth of a swarm flew to kadamba flowers, one-third to silindhra flowers, three times their difference to kutaja flowers, and one lonely bee stayed hovering. How many bees?
try it, then tap
Let the swarm be x. Three times the difference is 3(x/3 − x/5) = 2x/5. x/5 + x/3 + 2x/5 + 1 = x The fractions add up to 14x/15, so the one hovering bee is the missing 1/15 of the swarm: x = 15 bees. Check: 3 + 5 + 6 + 1 = 15. 🐝
✖️
First principles

One clue with two unknowns ("2 teas + 3 samosas cost ₹130") has endless answers: cheap tea and pricey samosas, or the reverse. On a graph it's a whole line of possibilities. A second clue is a second line. The only prices that satisfy both clues sit exactly where the lines cross.

Elimination is just al-muqābala from Act I, scaled up: make one item appear the same number of times on both receipts, then subtract one receipt from the other so that item cancels out. One unknown left, and you already know how to beat that. The same trick, run on thousands of equations at once, is what your phone's GPS, weather forecasts and spreadsheets do every second.

🧠 Recall before you scroll on
One equation, two unknowns. How many answers?
Infinitely many: a whole line of them. You need a second clue.
What does "elimination" actually do?
Scale the clues so one unknown matches, subtract, and it cancels, leaving one unknown.
Two receipts give parallel lines. What does it mean?
The clues contradict each other: no prices fit both. (Same line = the second clue added nothing.)

So far every unknown has stood alone: x, never x times x. But the moment area, speed or profit enters the story, x multiplies itself, and the straight lines bend into curves. Back to Baghdad, and that sentence about a square and ten roots.

ACT IV LEVEL 4

The Square

When x meets itself, lines become curves, and curves have a best point.

628 CE · BHINMAL, RAJASTHAN

Brahmagupta gives debts a place at the table

In his Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta, Brahmagupta does something radical: he treats zero and negative numbers as real numbers, with rules. He calls positives fortunes and negatives debts: a debt minus zero is a debt; the product of two debts is a fortune.

He also writes, in verse, a general rule for solving equations where the unknown is squared: the ancestor of the "quadratic formula" you may remember from school.

c. 820 CE · BAGHDAD, AGAIN

"A square and ten roots equal thirty-nine"

In symbols: x² + 10x = 39. Al-Khwārizmī doesn't have a formula. He has something better: a drawing. He takes the words literally. A "square" is a real square. "Roots" are its sides. Then he finishes the shape.

Animation · completing the square

Solve x² + 10x = 39 with scissors

Why is a debt times a debt a fortune? (Why is negative × negative positive?)
tap to reveal
Think of "×(−)" as taking away. Say you carry three ₹500 fines (debts: −500 each). The office cancels them: you remove (−3) of the (−500) debts. Your net worth goes up by ₹1,500. So (−3) × (−500) = +1,500. Removing a bad thing is a good thing. Brahmagupta saw this in 628.
🌀
First principles

A problem turns quadratic whenever a quantity gets multiplied by something that depends on itself: width × (a length that shrinks as width grows); price × (sales that fall as price rises); speed × speed. Those trade-offs make a hill-shaped curve, a parabola, and the top of the hill is the best possible answer.

Playground · the kitchen garden

40 metres of fencing. One wall for free. Biggest garden?

You want a vegetable patch against your compound wall. The wall forms one side, so you only fence three sides. Make it too thin and it's a corridor. Too wide and it's a shallow strip. Drag to find the best width.

🍱

Pricing a home tiffin

At ₹80 you sell 50 tiffins a day. Every ₹1 you add loses half a customer. Best price?

tap to solve
Raise by p: revenue = (80 + p)(50 − 0.5p). It's a parabola whose peak is at p = 10. Charge ₹90: 45 tiffins, ₹4,050 a day, more money for less cooking.
🚗

Braking distance

You double your speed from 40 to 80 km/h. How much longer to stop?

tap to solve
Braking distance grows with speed². Double the speed → 2² = 4× the distance. That's why 80 in a 40 zone isn't "twice" as risky. It's x² on the road.
🧮

The formula, finally

Do al-Khwārizmī's square-completing once, for every quadratic ax² + bx + c = 0…

tap to see
x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2aThe famous formula is just the scissors trick done in general. The ± is Brahmagupta's gift: the second answer can be negative.
🧠 Recall before you scroll on
What does "completing the square" literally mean?
Adding the missing corner piece, (b/2)², so the L-shape becomes a perfect square whose side you can read off.
When does a real problem become quadratic?
When the unknown multiplies something that depends on itself: area, price × sales, speed².
40 m fence, one wall free: best width and area?
Width 10 m, length 20 m, area 200 m². Twice as long as wide, because the wall is free.

x² is x multiplied by itself once. What happens when something multiplies itself again, and again, every month, for twenty years? That's your savings, your loan, and a king who lost a bet over a chessboard.

First principles

Linear growth adds the same amount each step (Act II: ₹18 more per km). Exponential growth multiplies by the same factor each step, so the bigger it gets, the faster it grows. Interest on a balance, a rumour, a virus, compounding returns: anything that grows in proportion to what it already is.

amount = start × (1 + r)n
1614 · EDINBURGH

Napier turns multiplication into addition

Astronomers spent months multiplying huge numbers by hand. Scottish laird John Napier spent some twenty years building tables that turned each multiplication into a simple addition. He called them logarithms. Laplace is often credited with saying they doubled astronomers' lives by halving their labour.

Strip away the history and a logarithm answers one question: "how many times do I multiply?" 2 multiplied by itself how many times makes 1,024? Ten. So log₂(1,024) = 10. When your question is "how long until my money doubles?", the unknown is in the exponent, and the logarithm is the tool that pulls it down.

⏳

The rule of 72

How long until something doubles at r% a year? Exactly: log 2 ÷ log(1 + r). In your head:

tap to see
years to double ≈ 72 ÷ rFD at 7% → ~10 years. At 12% → ~6 years.
🛒

Inflation, the silent thief

At 6% inflation, when does your ₹1 lakh buy only half of what it buys today?

tap to solve
72 ÷ 6 = ~12 years. (Exactly: log 2 ÷ log 1.06 ≈ 11.9.) Money sitting idle isn't safe; it's shrinking on a schedule.
💳

Credit-card debt

Unpaid card balance at ~3% a month. How fast does it double?

tap to solve
3% a month ≈ 42.6% a year compounded. 72 ÷ 42.6 ≈ under 2 years. Exponential growth works against you just as hard as it works for you.
The summit · derive the EMI formula

Every bank uses one equation. You can build it in four steps.

Borrow P at monthly rate r for n months. Find the fixed monthly payment E that brings the balance to exactly zero. Tap each step.

① What happens each month?

tap
The balance grows by interest, then your EMI is subtracted: Bnext = B × (1 + r) − E

② Unroll all n months

tap
Each EMI you pay also "would have grown" until the end. For the loan to hit zero, the loan grown for n months must equal all your EMIs grown to the end: P(1+r)n = E [ (1+r)n−1 + … + (1+r) + 1 ]

③ The geometric-series trick

tap
Call the bracket S with q = 1+r: S = 1 + q + … + qn−1. Multiply by q: qS = q + … + qn. Subtract, and everything in the middle cancels (al-muqābala again!): qS − S = qn − 1 → S = (qn − 1) / r

④ Solve for E

tap
E = P · r · (1+r)n / ((1+r)n − 1)That's the formula behind every home, car and personal loan in India. You just derived it from "do the same thing to both sides."

Why do early EMIs barely dent the loan, and why does prepaying early help so much?
tap to reveal
Interest is charged on the remaining balance, which is biggest at the start. Your EMI is fixed, so in the early years interest eats most of it. Every rupee you prepay early removes that rupee's interest for every remaining month: you're putting exponential growth to work for you instead of the bank. 🧠 Recall before you scroll on
Linear vs exponential, in one line each?
Linear adds the same amount each step. Exponential multiplies by the same factor each step.
What question does a logarithm answer?
"How many times do I multiply?" e.g. how many doublings, how many years.
The rule of 72?
Years to double ≈ 72 ÷ yearly % rate. 6% → 12 years.
The trick behind the EMI formula?
The geometric series: multiply S by q and subtract, and the middle terms cancel. S = (qⁿ − 1)/(q − 1).
