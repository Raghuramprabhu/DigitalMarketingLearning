The big picture in one line

India gave the world zero and negative numbers → scholars in Baghdad translated Indian maths and wrote the first algebra book → Europe translated that into Latin and, centuries later, invented the symbols (=, x) we use today. Algebra is a relay race across three civilisations.

BEHIND ACT I · THE BALANCE

Where the two moves came from

📖

al-Khwarizmi

c. 820 CE · Baghdad (in today's Iraq)
Say it: al-KHWAA-riz-mee · think of him as "the man whose name became 'algorithm'"

A Persian scholar at Baghdad's House of Wisdom, a giant library where people translated Greek, Persian and Indian books. He wrote a short, practical handbook for ordinary problems: splitting inheritances, measuring land, pricing trade.

It had no symbols at all, not even =. Every problem was a sentence. To solve them he used exactly two moves, which he named:

al-jabr"restoring": add back what was taken away, on both sides. (Kavya's 🦴 Restore.)
al-muqabala"balancing": remove the same thing from both sides. (Kavya's ⚖️ Balance.)
Remember him forThe first word of his move list, al-jabr, became algebra. His own name, in Latin, became algorithm.
Why did "algebra" once mean setting broken bones?
tap to reveal
Al-jabr literally means reuniting broken parts. In medieval Spain, an algebrista was a bone-setter, and one even appears in the novel Don Quixote. Solving an equation is the same idea: you "reset" a quantity that was broken off one side.

🟰

Robert Recorde

1557 · Wales
Say it: REK-ord · think of him as "the man who got tired of writing 'is equal to'"

For 700 years after the Baghdad book, people still wrote equations out in words. Recorde, a Welsh doctor who wrote maths textbooks, was fed up with writing "is equal to" again and again. So he invented a sign: two parallel lines of the same length, because, he said, no two things could be more equal.

Remember him forThe = sign. The promise at the heart of Act I has his signature on it.

BEHIND ACT II · THE LINE

The man who made equations visible

🪰

René Descartes

1637 · published in the Netherlands, born in France
Say it: reh-NAY day-KART · think of him as "the 'I think, therefore I am' philosopher who also drew the graph"

The famous philosopher added a maths appendix to his most famous book. Two small decisions in it shaped every maths class since:

LettersEnd of the alphabet (x, y, z) for unknowns; start (a, b, c) for known numbers. We still do this.
GraphsAny point on paper = two numbers (across, up). So an equation in x and y draws a shape. Kavya's cab and scooter lines exist because of this.

LEGEND The story goes that he got the idea lying in bed, watching a fly on the ceiling and realising its position could be described by its distance from two walls. Probably invented, but the idea is real. Another Frenchman, Pierre de Fermat, reached the same idea on his own at about the same time.

So why is the unknown called "x", of all letters?
tap to reveal
A popular story says the Arabic word shay' ("thing") became Spanish xei, shortened to x. Charming, but historians have found little evidence for it. Another tale says Descartes's printer had spare x's. Also unproven. The honest answer is boring: Descartes used the last letters for unknowns, and x simply became the favourite. A good thinker notices when the myth is more fun than the truth.

BEHIND ACT III · TWO UNKNOWNS

Counting rods and a poet's riddles

🧮

The "Nine Chapters"

c. 200 BCE – 200 CE · China · many unknown authors
No name to say: it's a book, written and edited by many hands over centuries.

The Chinese classic Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art solved problems like "3 bundles of good grain and 2 of poor grain yield 39 measures…" by laying out counting rods on a table in a grid, then cancelling one unknown at a time.

Remember it forThe receipts trick (elimination) is about 2,000 years old. Europe rediscovered it much later and named it after Gauss.

🐝

Bhaskara

1150 · Ujjain (in today's Madhya Pradesh)
Say it: BHAAS-ka-ra · also called Bhaskaracharya · think of him as "the astronomer who wrote maths as poetry"

Head of the observatory at Ujjain, he wrote a maths book in Sanskrit verse called Lilavati (say: LEE-laa-va-tee). Its problems are addressed to a young girl and full of bees, lotuses, peacocks, pearls and merchants.

LEGEND Astrologers fixed one lucky moment for Lilavati's wedding, timed by a cup with a hole slowly sinking in water. A pearl fell from her jewellery, blocked the hole, and the moment silently passed. To console her, her father named his book after her. Lovely and unprovable.

Remember him forMaking maths beautiful and story-driven, 900 years before this website tried.
Bhaskara's bee riddle: one-fifth of a swarm flew to one kind of flower, one-third to another, three times their difference to a third kind, and one lonely bee stayed hovering. How many bees?
try it, then tap
Let the swarm be x. Three times the difference is 3(x/3 − x/5) = 2x/5. x/5 + x/3 + 2x/5 + 1 = x The fractions add up to 14x/15, so the one hovering bee is the missing 1/15 of the swarm: x = 15 bees. Check: 3 + 5 + 6 + 1 = 15. 🐝

BEHIND ACT IV · THE SQUARE

Debts, fortunes, and the tiler's real ancestor

🇮🇳

Brahmagupta

628 CE · Bhinmal (in today's Rajasthan)
Say it: BRAH-ma-GOOP-ta · think of him as "the man who gave zero and negatives their rules"

An astronomer who did something radical: he treated zero and negative numbers as real numbers with rules. He called positives fortunes and negatives debts. "A debt minus zero is a debt." "The product of two debts is a fortune." (That's the Act IV twist: negative × negative = positive.)

Remember him forRules for zero and negative numbers, and a general rule (in verse!) for solving equations with x², the ancestor of the school quadratic formula.
↩ back to Act IV
📖

The tiler's puzzle is 1,200 years old. In the story, the tiler laid 39 m² for a square room plus a 10 m verandah: x² + 10x = 39. Those are the exact numbers from al-Khwarizmi's book, which says it in words: "a square and ten roots equal thirty-nine." He solved it by drawing the square and completing the missing corner, exactly like the animation. He ignored the negative answer (−13) because, for him, a length couldn't be negative. Brahmagupta, two centuries earlier, would have accepted it as a "debt".

BEHIND ACT V · GROWTH

A legend and a laird

♟️

LEGEND Grandpa's chessboard story has been retold for centuries in India, Persia and beyond, with different names for the inventor and the king. Nobody knows if it happened. It survives because it's the best explanation of exponential growth ever invented.

📈

John Napier

1614 · Scotland
Say it: NAY-pee-er · think of him as "the man who turned multiplying into adding"

A Scottish landowner (a "laird") who spent about twenty years building tables of logarithms. Astronomers spent months multiplying giant numbers by hand; his tables turned each multiplication into a simple addition. A later astronomer reportedly said logarithms doubled astronomers' lives by halving their work.

Remember him forLogarithms: the tool that answers "how many times do I multiply?", like "how many years until my money doubles?"

🧠 Seven names, seven hooks
Whose name became the word "algorithm"?
al-Khwarizmi (al-KHWAA-riz-mee), Baghdad, ~820. His book also gave us "algebra".
Who gave zero and negative numbers their rules?
Brahmagupta (BRAH-ma-GOOP-ta), Rajasthan, 628. Negatives = "debts".
Who wrote maths as poetry, named after a girl?
Bhaskara (BHAAS-ka-ra), Ujjain, 1150. The book: Lilavati.
Who invented the "=" sign?
Robert Recorde, Wales, 1557. Two equal parallel lines.
Who turned equations into graphs?
René Descartes, 1637. Also why we use x and y.
Who invented logarithms?
John Napier, Scotland, 1614. Multiplying → adding.

Seven people, three civilisations, two thousand years, and every one of them was trying to solve a real problem. Just like Kavya.
