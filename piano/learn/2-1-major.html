Level 2 · Lesson 1 · The first scale

One scale, three names:
major · Bilawal · Shankarabharanam

The most-used scale on Earth is a recipe: a pattern of big and small steps. Learn the pattern and you can build it from any key, in any tradition.

By the end you'll be able to

  • Tell a half step from a whole step on the keyboard and by ear.
  • Build the major scale from any starting note using W-W-H-W-W-W-H.
  • Name its notes as scale degrees, in solfège, Hindustani sargam and Carnatic codes.
  • Explain why the same tune can start on any key (transposition), and why sargam makes that effortless.

Play only the white keys from C to C. You've just played the major scale. In Hindustani music it's Bilawal, and when Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande organised Hindustani ragas into ten families (thaats) in the early 1900s, he made Bilawal the reference: its notes are the shuddha ("pure") swaras. In Carnatic music it's the 29th melakarta, Dheerasankarabharanam, usually just called Shankarabharanam, "the ornament of Shiva".

Here's the twist: the white keys are only "natural" because the piano was designed around this scale. Start on D and play only white keys, and you get a different scale. So the scale isn't really "the white keys". It's the pattern of gaps between them.

1 · The two sizes of step

On a keyboard, the smallest step is from one key to the very next key, black or white: a half step (semitone). Two half steps make a whole step (tone). Look at the white keys: most have a black key between them (whole steps), but E–F and B–C touch directly (half steps).

2 · The recipe: W W H W W W H

Walk from C to C on the white keys and write down each gap: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. That's the major scale. Any start note + this pattern = a major scale.

W W H W W W H

🏗 Build it from any key

Choose a start note above. Try D or G, then compare "white keys only". Hear how wrong it sounds when the pattern breaks?

Build the major scale on G in your head, using W W H W W W H. Which note needs a black key?
G A B C D E F♯ G. The 7th step must be a whole step above E, so F becomes F♯. That's why the key of G major has one sharp. (Check it with the builder above.)

3 · Two matching halves

Split the scale in the middle and something neat appears: S R G M and P D N Ṡ have the same shape, W-W-H, joined by one whole step (M → P).

Western

Each 4-note half is a tetrachord (from Greek, "four strings"). The major scale = two identical major tetrachords, a whole step apart.

Hindustani

The lower half (Sa to Ma/Pa) is the purvang, and the upper half (Pa to taar Sa) is the uttarang. Ragas are often described as "purvang-pradhan" or "uttarang-pradhan", depending on which half they live in.

Notice "leading tone": Ni sits a half step under Ṡa and pulls toward it. Play the scale and stop on Ni: you'll feel the itch to finish.

A surprise in the history

Bilawal hasn't always been India's "pure" scale. In older Indian theory, the basic scale (the shadja grama) was closer to what we'd now call Kafi (Kharaharapriya: komal Ga and komal Ni). Bhatkhande's choice of Bilawal as the modern reference brought Hindustani terminology closer to the Western major scale. Carnatic teaching, meanwhile, starts beginners on Mayamalavagowla (as you heard in Lesson 1.3), not Shankarabharanam.

5 · Same tune, any Sa

Here's a tune everyone knows (the French melody behind "Twinkle Twinkle", from 1761): S S P P D D P – | M M G G R R S –. Because it's written in sargam, it doesn't say which key to play. Pick a Sa and it just works. That's transposition: moving a whole tune up or down while keeping its shape.

🔁 Transpose it

Letters change with every Sa. The sargam stays S S P P D D P. That's why Indian musicians learn songs in sargam.

6 · Check yourself

Which pairs of white keys are only a half step apart?

There's no black key between E and F, or between B and C. Those two half steps are exactly where the scale's "H"s fall when you start on C.

The major scale on D is…

W W H W W W H from D: D→E (W) →F♯ (W) →G (H) →A (W) →B (W) →C♯ (W) →D (H). The white-keys-only version (D E F G A B C D) is a different scale: Dorian, i.e. Kafi / Kharaharapriya. You'll meet it in Level 5.

In Carnatic codes, Shankarabharanam is:

R1 G3 … D1 N3 is Mayamalavagowla. G2 and N2 give Kharaharapriya. M2 (tivra Ma) gives Mechakalyani (Kalyani), the Lydian mode, Hindustani Kalyan/Yaman.

Why does the scale's 7th note (Ni / Ti) feel like it wants to move?

A half step is the smallest, most "magnetic" move. That pull is why Western theory calls it the leading tone. Ragas that use komal Ni lose that pull, which is part of their different mood.

8 · Ear test: is it major?

You'll hear a scale going up. Either it's a clean major scale, or one note has been bent (made komal or tivra). Trust the itch.

Takeaways

  • Half step = next key. Whole step = two half steps.
  • Major scale = W W H W W W H from any note. = Bilawal (all shuddha swaras) = Shankarabharanam (S R2 G3 M1 P D2 N3, mela 29).
  • Two identical halves (tetrachords / purvang + uttarang), each W-W-H, joined by a whole step.
  • Degrees: tonic · supertonic · mediant · subdominant · dominant · submediant · leading tone = Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni = Do Re Mi Fa Sol La Ti.
  • Sargam is built for transposition: the names describe relationships, so a tune is the same on any Sa.
