Beat, tala & time signature

Pitch is how often the air is pushed. Rhythm is the same idea, slowed down until you can count it. Here's how both traditions turn a steady pulse into a cycle you can feel in your body.

By the end you'll be able to

  • Explain beat, tempo (BPM) and laya, and the Carnatic "speeds" (kalam).
  • Count 4/4, 3/4 and 6/8, and keep Teental, Adi and Rupak with your hands.
  • Explain sam, tali and khali, and why a tala is a cycle rather than a ruler.
  • Measure how accurately you clap on the beat.

Walk into a Carnatic concert and watch the audience, not the stage. Hundreds of people are quietly keeping time on their thighs: a clap, three finger taps, clap, wave, clap, wave. Nobody's conducting them. When the singer and the mridangam player land a long, tricky phrase exactly on beat one of the cycle, the whole hall exhales, and sometimes applauds.

In a Western orchestra, one person does that job for everyone: the conductor, whose baton traces the same shapes (down, left, right, up for 4 beats) that the audience in Chennai traces with their hands. Two traditions, one instinct: time is something you show with your body.

1 · The beat and its speed

A beat is the steady pulse you'd tap your foot to. Tempo is how fast it goes, measured in beats per minute (BPM). A resting heart is about 60–80 BPM, and so is a calm song.

Western

Tempo words in Italian: largo (very slow), adagio (slow), andante (walking), moderato, allegro (fast), presto (very fast). Or simply a number: ♩ = 96.

Indian

Laya is tempo. Hindustani: vilambit (slow), madhya (medium), drut (fast). A khayal often starts in vilambit and speeds up. Carnatic adds kalam (speeds): the same melody sung at 1, 2 and 4 notes per beat, while the tala's beat stays put.

2 · Grouping beats: meter and tala

Beats on their own are like an endless row of fence posts. Music groups them. The first beat of each group feels heavier, and that weight is what makes a waltz feel different from a march.

Western: the time signature

Written like a fraction at the start of the music. 4/4 = four beats per bar (strong, weak, medium, weak). 3/4 = three (strong, weak, weak: the waltz). 6/8 = six quick beats felt as two groups of three. Bars repeat like identical boxes.

Indian: the tala

A tala is a cycle (avartan / avartanam) that comes back round, divided into sections (vibhag in Hindustani, anga in Carnatic). Its first beat is sam, the "home" of time, where phrases resolve. Hindustani marks some sections with a clap (tali) and one with a wave (khali, "empty").

Teental has 16 beats in four groups of 4. Which beat is sam, and which group is khali?
Beat 1 is sam (X). The third group (beats 9–12) is khali (0), played with open, lighter strokes: "Ta Tin Tin Ta" instead of "Dha Dhin Dhin Dha". That "empty" section tells listeners where they are in the cycle, so they can feel sam coming.

Rupak's puzzle

Play Rupak above and look at beat 1. In every other Hindustani tala, sam is a clap. In Rupak, sam falls on the khali, the "empty" wave. So the most important beat is marked by the lightest gesture. Musicians argue about why. It's a good reminder that a tala isn't just counting: it's a shape with a character.

3 · Carnatic hands: Adi tala

Adi ("first") tala is the most common Carnatic tala: 8 beats, built from three angas:

  • Laghu (4 beats): a clap, then count with the little, ring and middle fingers.
  • Drutam (2 beats): clap, wave.
  • Drutam (2 beats): clap, wave.

Now let's do what every Carnatic student does in week one: Sarali Varisai, the first exercise attributed to Purandara Dasa (16th century), who organised how Carnatic music is taught. It's sung in raga Mayamalavagowla (S R1 G3 M1 P D1 N3), over Adi tala, first at 1 note per beat, then 2, then 4: the three kalams.

🎼 Sarali Varisai 1 in three speeds

Notes: S R G M | P D | N Ṡ ‖ Ṡ N D P | M G | R S. Watch the tala strip above. At 2nd speed the whole exercise fits in one cycle, and at 3rd speed it repeats twice per cycle. The beat never changes. Only the density does.

Where they meet

Carnatic "speeds" are the same idea as Western note values: quarter notes (1 per beat), eighth notes (2 per beat), sixteenth notes (4 per beat). A Western drummer would call 3rd speed "sixteenths". Same maths, different words. You'll write it both ways in Lesson 4.1.

4 · Clap-along test

Start the tala player (any tala, any tempo), then tap the button, or press Space, on every beat. You'll see whether you rush (early) or drag (late), in milliseconds. Musicians usually land within about ±30 ms. Most beginners rush.

5 · Check yourself

In Hindustani music, "khali" means…

Khali is still played, just with lighter, open strokes (no bass drum). It's a landmark that helps everyone find sam.

A singer goes from 1st speed to 3rd speed in Adi tala. What happens?

Kalam doubles the density each step: 1 → 2 → 4 notes per beat. The tala (and the audience's hands) don't change at all.

Which pair describes the same idea in the two traditions?

Both group beats with a strong first beat. The difference is emphasis: a Western bar is a box that repeats, while a tala is a cycle whose "return home" to sam is a big musical event.

Adi tala, counted by hand, goes:

Laghu (clap + little, ring, middle finger) + drutam (clap, wave) + drutam (clap, wave) = 4 + 2 + 2 = 8 beats.

6 · Ear test: how long is the cycle?

You'll hear a pulse with an accent on beat one, repeated three times. Count the beats from one accent to the next.

Takeaways

  • Beat = steady pulse. Tempo/laya = its speed (BPM; vilambit · madhya · drut).
  • Meter groups beats into identical bars (4/4, 3/4, 6/8). A tala is a cycle (avartan) with sections (vibhag/anga) and a home beat, sam.
  • Hindustani: tali (clap) and khali (wave). Teental 16 = 4+4+4+4 with khali on 9. Rupak puts sam on khali.
  • Carnatic: Adi tala = laghu (4) + drutam (2) + drutam (2). Kalam = 1, 2, 4 notes per beat, the same idea as quarter, eighth and sixteenth notes.
