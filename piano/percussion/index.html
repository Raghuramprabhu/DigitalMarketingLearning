A thought to start: when the Sai bhajan movement began, the singing had no instruments at all. Rhythm lived in the voice and the clapping hands. Everything below grows from that: first the clap, then the drum.

Tambourine (jingle tambourine). Hold the frame in your left hand, jingles free.
  • Hit: strike the centre into your right palm. The loud crash, saved for the strong beats.
  • Tap: fingertips near the rim. A light jingle for the in-between beats.
  • Shake: a quick wrist twist. The sizzle that fills the gaps.
  • Roll: run a damp thumb around the edge. A shimmer for endings.
Dholak. A barrel drum held across the lap. The two heads are tuned a fourth or fifth apart.
  • Left hand, bass head (coated inside, so it booms): Ge = fingers bounce off, open boom. Press with the heel of your hand afterwards to bend the pitch up. Ka = flat-palm slap that stays on the skin.
  • Right hand, treble head: Na/Ta = fingertips near the edge, a bright crack. Tin = fingers further in, softer. Te = fingers stay down, dry. Tu = open centre.
  • Dha = Na + Ge together. Dhin = Tin + Ge.
Dholki (naal). Longer and narrower than a dholak, with tighter heads: higher, sharper, more "crack". It's the drum of Marathi lavani and bhajani. Same bol language as the dholak: Ge and Ka on the bass head, Na, Tin, Te and Tu on the treble head, Dha and Dhin together.
Tabla. The right drum (dayan) is tuned to your Sa (pick it above). The left drum (bayan) is the bass.
  • Na/Ta: index finger strikes the rim while the ring finger rests lightly at the edge of the black patch (syahi). Bright, ringing.
  • Tin: index finger on the open band between rim and syahi. Softer ring.
  • Tu: index finger strikes the syahi centre and bounces off. Deep, open.
  • Ti/Te: middle fingers strike the syahi and stay. Closed, dry.
  • Ge: left fingers bounce on the bayan, wrist resting. Slide the wrist to bend the pitch. Ka: flat left palm, stays down.
  • Dha = Na + Ge. Dhin = Tin + Ge.

Why the tabla sings and the dholak thumps

Hit a plain drum skin and it vibrates in several shapes at once. For a round skin, those shapes vibrate at frequencies of about 1, 1.59, 2.14, 2.30 and 2.65 times the lowest one. Those numbers aren't whole-number multiples, so the ear hears a "thud" with no clear note. That's the dholak and dholki.

The tabla's black syahi changes the physics. It's a carefully built-up, heavier patch that loads the skin, so the shapes line up at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 times the lowest frequency, exactly like a string. Whole-number overtones = a definite pitch. That's why a tabla is tuned to Sa, and why the mridangam is too. C. V. Raman studied this in Indian drums in the 1920s, years before his Nobel Prize for light.

Connect it

This is the same "ratio" story as Lesson 1.1 in Music: the story. Whole-number ratios = a clear pitch and consonance. Messy ratios = noise and thud. A drum maker who builds up a syahi in layers is doing physics by ear.

Playing in a bhajan group

  • Come in after the singer and the drum. The lead singer starts, then harmonium, then tabla or dholak, and the tambourine and bells come in last. Same again each time the bhajan speeds up.
  • Follow the drum, not the other way round. The tambourine is loud: if you rush, everyone hears it.
  • Simple beats busy. Level 1 (steady) played rock-solid is worth more than Level 3 played shakily. If you lose the beat, stop cleanly and come back in on sam.
  • Bhajans speed up, usually in steps. Practise with "Speed up like a bhajan" switched on so the jump doesn't throw you.
  • Play soft. The rhythm is a vessel for the singing, not a performance of its own.

Check yourself

In Keherwa, where do you clap and where do you wave?

8 beats = 4 + 4. The first half opens with the clap (sam, X) and the second half opens with the wave (khali, 0). The theka changes there too: "Na Ka Dhi Na" has fewer bass strokes.

On a tabla, Dha is…

Most "dh-" bols are combinations: Dha = Na + Ge, Dhin = Tin + Ge. Bols with an "h" usually mean the bass drum is involved.

Why can you tune a tabla to Sa but not really a dholak?

Plain skins ring at inharmonic ratios (1, 1.59, 2.14…). The loaded syahi pulls them into 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, the same pattern a string makes, so the ear hears a definite note.
