Level 1 · Lesson 2 · Names

C D E meets Sa Re Ga

Two naming systems for the same twelve keys. One gives every key a fixed address. The other names notes by their relationship to home. Once you see why both exist, you'll never confuse them again.

By the end you'll be able to

  • Find any note on a keyboard instantly, using only the black-key groups.
  • Explain fixed names (C D E) vs movable names (Sa Re Ga), and set Sa on any key.
  • Name all 12 positions in Western, Hindustani and Carnatic terms.
  • Write and read the three registers (mandra, madhya, taar).

Around the year 1025, an Italian monk called Guido of Arezzo had a problem: choirboys took years to learn chants by ear. His trick was a hymn whose lines each started one step higher. He took the first syllable of each line, Ut, Re, Mi, Fa, Sol, La, and suddenly the steps had names you could sing. Ut later became Do, and Ti was added. That's where "Do-Re-Mi" comes from.

India had already done this, much earlier. The seven swaras each have a Sanskrit name: Sadja, Rishabha, Gandhara, Madhyama, Panchama, Dhaivata, Nishada. Sing just their first syllables and you get sa-re-ga-ma: the word sargam literally comes from the first four notes. Tradition even links each swara to an animal call. Pa is the koel (cuckoo), and Sa is the peacock.

Notice the twist: both civilisations ended up singing a syllable per step, and both have a note called "Re".

1 · The keyboard is a map with landmarks

Ignore the white keys for a second and look at the black ones. They come in a repeating pattern: a group of 2, then a group of 3. Those groups are your landmarks.

  • C is the white key just left of every group of 2.
  • F is the white key just left of every group of 3.

2 · Fixed names: letters

Western music gives every white key a letter, A to G, repeating. They're fixed addresses: C is always C, no matter what song you play. Middle C is C4 (about 261.6 Hz), the next C up is C5, and so on.

The black keys borrow names from their neighbours. The key between C and D is C♯ ("C sharp", a step up from C) or D♭ ("D flat", a step down from D). Same key, two names, depending on context. Those are enharmonic spellings.

3 · Movable names: sargam

Sargam does something cleverer. Sa is not a key. Sa is "home", wherever the singer decides home is. Everything else is named by its distance from Sa. If Sa is C, then Re is D. If Sa is D, then Re is E.

🏠 Choose where Sa lives

Try Sa on C, then on G. The tune sounds the same, just higher or lower. That's the point: singers pick a Sa that suits their voice, and the music moves with them. Hindustani and Carnatic musicians name the pitch of their Sa in two different ways:

"Safed" means white key and "Kali" means black key, counted from C. So a singer who says "my Sa is Kali 1" or "1½ kattai" means C♯.

Where they meet

The West has a movable system too: movable-do solfège (Do Re Mi), where Do is whatever key you're in. That's sargam's idea exactly. Western musicians mostly think in fixed letters. Indian musicians mostly think in movable swaras. That single difference explains a lot: Indian music is about relationships to Sa, and Western music is about which key you're in.

Sa is set to G. Which letter is Pa? Which letter is Ma?
Pa is a fifth above Sa: G → D. Ma is a fourth above Sa: G → C. (Count the steps on the keyboard above with Sa on G to check.)

4 · All twelve positions

Sargam has seven names, but there are twelve keys. The extra five are altered versions of the movable swaras. (Sa and Pa never change: they're achala, immovable, as you saw in Lesson 1.1.)

Hindustani

  • Shuddha = natural. The seven swaras of the major scale.
  • Komal = soft (flattened): Re, Ga, Dha, Ni can each be komal. Written in lowercase here: r g d n.
  • Tivra = sharp: only Ma. Written M (or Ma′).

Carnatic

  • Each movable swara has numbered versions: R1 R2 R3, G1 G2 G3, M1 M2, D1 D2 D3, N1 N2 N3.
  • That's 16 names for 12 keys, because some share a key: R2 = G1, R3 = G2, D2 = N1, D3 = N2.
  • Why bother? It keeps the rule "Re comes before Ga" true in every scale. You'll see the payoff in the 72 melakartas (Lesson 5.3).

Careful with one trap: Hindustani "shuddha" Re is the natural Re (D when Sa = C), but Carnatic "Shuddha Rishabham" (R1) is the lowest Re (C♯). Same word, different meaning. That's why we'll use the short codes (R vs R1) from now on.

👂 Hear all twelve, both ways

With Sa = C, what's the Hindustani name and the Carnatic code for E♭? And for F♯?
E♭ = komal Ga (g) = Carnatic G2 (Sadharana Gandharam). F♯ = tivra Ma (M) = Carnatic M2 (Prati Madhyamam).

5 · Registers: the three saptaks

Letters handle octaves with numbers (C3, C4, C5). Sargam uses dots: a dot below a swara means the lower register (mandra), no dot means the middle register (madhya), and a dot above means the upper register (taar, or Carnatic tara). Both Hindustani and Carnatic notation use these dots.

⬆⬇ Three registers around Sa = C4

Look for the dots under the low notes (mandra P D N) and over the high ones (taar Ṡ Ṙ Ġ).

6 · Check yourself

You see a group of three black keys. The white key just to the left of it is…

C sits left of the 2-group, F left of the 3-group. Everything else follows alphabetically.

A singer sets Sa on D. Which key is komal Ga?

Komal Ga is 3 semitones above Sa. D → D♯ → E → F. (With Sa on C it would be E♭: movable names move!)

Why does Carnatic music have 16 swara names for only 12 keys?

It's spelling, like C♯ vs D♭ in the West. The name depends on the note's job in the scale, not just on its pitch.

A Carnatic singer says "my shruti is 5 kattai". Where is their Sa?

Kattai counts white keys from C: 1 = C, 2 = D, 3 = E, 4 = F, 5 = G. Half-steps are written ½.

Takeaways

  • Landmarks: C is left of each 2-group of black keys, and F is left of each 3-group.
  • Letters are fixed addresses. Sargam names are relationships to Sa, and Sa can sit on any key (kattai / safed-kali tells you which).
  • 12 positions: 7 shuddha + komal r g d n + tivra M. Carnatic codes R1–3, G1–3, M1–2, D1–3, N1–3 (16 names, because some keys get two).
  • Registers: mandra (dot below) · madhya · taar (dot above), versus C3 · C4 · C5.
