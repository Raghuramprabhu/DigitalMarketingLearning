Level 1 · Lesson 1 · Sound itself

What is a note?

Before names, scales or ragas, there is only air being pushed. Here's how a push becomes a note, why some notes feel like "the same note again", and where the number 12 really comes from.

By the end you'll be able to

  • Explain a note as a frequency, and an octave as "double it".
  • Say why Sa–Pa (the fifth) is the second-strongest bond in music, East and West.
  • Rebuild the 12 keys from scratch by stacking fifths, and explain the tiny gap that forced a compromise.
  • Tell an octave from any other leap by ear.

Two stories, both about strings. In Greece, legend says Pythagoras walked past a blacksmith's shop. Some hammers clanged together sweetly, others harshly. He went home, stretched strings, and found that the sweet pairs were lengths in simple ratios: 2 : 1, 3 : 2, 4 : 3. (The hammer part is physically wrong, but the ratio part is right, and it shaped Western music for 2,500 years.)

In India, the Natyashastra, attributed to Bharata and written roughly 2,000 years ago, describes an experiment with two identical veenas. One stays fixed (the dhruva veena). The other (the chala veena) is retuned down in tiny steps and compared, over and over. From this he counted 22 shrutis, the smallest pitch steps the ear can use inside an octave.

Two people, two continents, the same method: compare one vibrating string with another, and listen to the relationship. That's still the whole game.

1 · A note is a repeat count

Pluck a string and it swings back and forth, shoving the air each time. Your eardrum gets pushed in the same rhythm. If the pushes come regularly, your brain hears a pitch. If they're random, you hear noise (wind, a "shhh").

The number of pushes per second is the frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). Human ears catch roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. More pushes per second = higher note. The A above middle C is fixed by international agreement at 440 Hz.

At what ratio did the two tones melt into "the same note, higher"? And what's the second place where they sounded unusually sweet?
At ×2 (440 Hz against 220). That's the octave. The second sweet spot is at ×1.5 (330 Hz): the fifth, called Pa in Indian music. If you heard a calm blend near ×1.33 too, that's the fourth (Ma).

2 · The octave: double it, and it's "the same" note

Here's a strange fact your brain treats as obvious: 220 Hz and 440 Hz are different pitches, yet we give them the same name. Why? Look at the push patterns. Every second push of the 440 lines up exactly with a push of the 220. The two waves agree all the time, so the ear files them together.

Western

Calls the distance an octave (Latin octo, eight) because it's the 8th note of the scale: C D E F G A B C. Notes an octave apart share a letter. Middle C is C4, the one above is C5.

Indian

The seven notes S R G M P D N form a saptak (a "group of seven"), and the 8th is Sa again, one register up. Registers: mandra (lower), madhya (middle), taar (upper; Carnatic says tara sthayi). A dot above a swara means taar: Ṡ.

Where they meet

Both systems count seven named notes and then start again. "Octave" counts the arrival note (8). "Saptak" counts the seven inside. Same physics, different bookkeeping.

3 · The fifth: Sa and Pa

After ×2, the next simplest ratio is ×3/2 = ×1.5. The waves line up every 3 pushes of one and 2 of the other: still very regular, so it sounds stable and open. In the West it's the perfect fifth (C → G). In India it's Sa → Pa. And Panchama literally means "fifth".

That's why Pa is achala (immovable) in Indian theory: unlike Re, Ga, Dha, Ni, it never gets a komal or tivra version. And it's why the tanpura, the drone behind almost every Indian classical performance, is usually tuned Pa – Sa – Sa – Sa. Listen:

The fifth turned upside down is the fourth (×4/3): Sa → Ma. Go up a fifth from Ma and you land on Sa again. The West names this note the subdominant, the "dominant below", because it sits a fifth under the tonic just as the dominant (G, Pa) sits a fifth above. Madhyama means "the middle" one.

Why does Pa have no komal or tivra form, when Re, Ga, Dha and Ni all do?
Because Pa is the fifth, the simplest relationship after the octave (3 : 2). It's a fixed pillar, like Sa itself. That's why Sa and Pa are called achala swaras (immovable notes). Ma, the next-strongest (4 : 3), is allowed one alternative: tivra Ma.

4 · Why 12? Build the keyboard yourself

Here's a puzzle a Greek and an Indian would both have enjoyed. Start on C (Sa). Go up a fifth (×1.5) to G. From G, another fifth: D. Keep going. Whenever you fly past double, drop an octave (÷2) to stay in range. How many fifths until you come back home to C?

🔄 Stack fifths and see where you land

Start: C · ratio 1.0000 · 261.63 Hz

Twelve fifths later you're almost back on C, but not quite. You're about 1.36% sharp: 23.5 cents (a cent is 1/100 of a piano semitone). That gap is called the Pythagorean comma. Hear it:

That wobble ("beating", about 3–4 times a second) is two almost-equal frequencies drifting in and out of step. Nature doesn't close the circle. So to build an instrument that works in every key, people cheat: shave every fifth a hair (from ×1.5 to ×1.4983, just 2 cents), so 12 of them land exactly on 7 octaves. Each of the 12 steps then becomes the same size: ×21/12 ≈ ×1.0595. That compromise is equal temperament, and it's how a piano, and a harmonium, is tuned.

Where they meet, and where they part

The 12 comes from the fifth. Twelve fifths almost equal seven octaves (1.512 ≈ 129.7, and 27 = 128). That near-miss is why both Western and Indian music use 12 pitch positions per octave (Carnatic calls them the 12 swarasthanas).

But Indian music never fully accepted the cheat. A singer with a tanpura tunes each note to the drone by ear, which pulls it toward pure ratios, and Bharata's 22 shrutis describe finer shades than 12 keys can hold. This is one reason All India Radio banned the harmonium from 1940 to 1971 (and even then allowed it back only as accompaniment): its fixed, tempered notes, unable to glide, were seen as un-Indian. Fun twist: the first exact maths for equal temperament came from China. Prince Zhu Zaiyu published it in 1584, one year before Simon Stevin worked it out in Europe.

Explain to an imaginary friend, in two sentences, why a piano has 12 keys per octave and not 10 or 15.
Stacking the most natural interval, the fifth (×1.5), twelve times brings you almost exactly back to the starting note seven octaves up, so 12 is where the cycle (nearly) closes. Equal temperament shaves each fifth slightly so the cycle closes perfectly, giving 12 equal steps.

5 · Check yourself

A string vibrates at 300 Hz. Which frequency is the same note one octave higher?

Octave = ×2. 450 Hz is ×1.5, a fifth (Pa above it). 900 Hz is ×3: an octave plus a fifth.

If Sa is 240 Hz, roughly what is Pa?

Pa is the fifth: ×3/2 = 360 Hz. (320 Hz is ×4/3, which is Ma. 300 Hz is ×5/4, which is Ga in pure tuning.)

Why can't you tune all twelve fifths perfectly pure and still return exactly to your starting note?

The comma (~23.5 cents) is pure arithmetic: powers of 3 never equal powers of 2. Every tuning system is a way of hiding that gap somewhere.

Which statement best describes how Indian classical music relates to the 12 equal steps of a keyboard?

The 12 swarasthanas are the "map". The shrutis and tuning-by-ear are the "territory". Shrutis are not equal-sized steps, and the octave is central to both systems.

6 · Ear test: octave or not?

You'll hear two notes, one after the other. Are they the same note an octave apart, or two different notes? (Tip: an octave sounds "hollow" and settled. A different note sounds like the melody has gone somewhere.)

Takeaways

  • A note is a frequency: regular pushes of air per second. A = 440 Hz is the agreed reference.
  • ×2 = octave: same note name, higher. West: C4 → C5. India: madhya Sa → taar Ṡ (a saptak later).
  • ×1.5 = fifth = Sa → Pa (Panchama, "the fifth"). Pa and Sa are achala, so the tanpura is tuned Pa–Sa–Sa–Sa.
  • Twelve fifths ≈ seven octaves → 12 notes per octave. The leftover gap is the Pythagorean comma.
  • Equal temperament spreads that gap evenly (each step ×1.0595). Indian music keeps the 12 positions, but its ear tuning and its 22-shruti theory go finer.
