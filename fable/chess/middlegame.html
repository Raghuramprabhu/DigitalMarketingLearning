THE FIRST PRINCIPLE

How does a general win before the first shot?

Before a battle, a good general doesn't count bullets. He studies three things.

The terrain: rivers, hills, bridges. They don't move, and they decide where armies can go. The army: troops are strong where the terrain suits them and useless where it doesn't, so cavalry on a mountain is wasted. And the mind of the enemy: what is he trying to do, and how long will it take him?

A chess middlegame is the same three layers. Over two hundred years ago François-André Philidor wrote that pawns are the soul of chess. He meant this: pawns are the terrain. They can never move backwards, so every pawn move permanently redraws the map. The pieces are the army, and the plan is the mind.

Principle 01 · read the terrain

The pawn skeleton

You can move the furniture any time. You can't move the walls.

Renovating a house, you can shuffle sofas and tables all day. But the load-bearing walls were decided when the house was built, and they decide where every room can be.

Pieces are furniture: a knight can go back to where it came from. Pawns are walls: a pawn move is forever. That's why the pawn structure, more than anything else, tells you where your pieces belong and which side of the board to play on.

First principle

Pawns can't retreat, so the pawn structure is the only permanent thing in the middlegame. Play on the side where you have more pawns (a majority) or where your pawn chains point. And treat every pawn move as a decision you'll live with for the rest of the game.

A pawn majority on one side: that's where you can create a passed pawn.
Pawn chains point at the side you should attack.
Releasing central tension hands your opponent a free centre.
Why is a pawn move the most dangerous kind of move in chess?
tap to reveal
It's the only move that can never be undone (apart from a capture). And every pawn advance leaves behind squares that pawn can never guard again. Each one creates a weakness. Strong players move pawns rarely, and only with a purpose.
Principle 02 · read the terrain

Outposts

An embassy on enemy soil. It can't be evicted.

An embassy sits in a foreign capital, and the host country's police can't walk in. It watches everything around it, and the only way to get rid of it is a full diplomatic trade.

An outpost is a square in the enemy's half that no enemy pawn can ever attack. A piece standing there (usually a knight) can only be removed by trading a piece for it. Until then it stares into the enemy camp, all game long.

First principle

Pawns capture only diagonally forward. Once the enemy pawns on the neighbouring files are gone, or have advanced past a square, that square is weak forever. Holes are the price of pawn moves, and your pieces should collect that price.

A square in front of an enemy pawn whose neighbouring pawns are gone or advanced.
A knight route to that square (knights love the 5th and 6th ranks).
The enemy piece that could trade off your outpost piece. Remove that one first.
Why are outposts mostly for knights, not bishops or rooks?
tap to reveal
A knight is short-range: it must stand close to the action to matter, and it needs a stable square because it can be chased easily. Bishops and rooks work from far away along lines, so they don't need to stand in the enemy's half. A knight on a protected 6th-rank square hits eight squares in the enemy camp.
Principle 03 · fit the army

Pieces must fit the pawns

A sports car on a mountain trail is a bad car.

A Ferrari is a wonderful car on a highway. On a rocky mountain path, a rusty jeep beats it. The car didn't change. The road did.

Bishops and knights are "worth three pawns" only on paper. A bishop whose own pawns stand on its colour is a Ferrari stuck behind a wall: a bad bishop. A knight with no outposts and pawns on both wings is a jeep on a racetrack. Their real value is set by the pawns around them.

First principle

A piece's strength is the number of useful squares it controls, and the pawns decide those squares. Put your pawns on the opposite colour from your bishop. Keep the minor piece that fits the structure (bishops for open boards with pawns on both wings, knights for closed boards with outposts), even if it means giving up the prettier one.

Count your pawns on your bishop's colour. Many pawns there means it's a bad bishop.
Pawns on both wings and an open board favour the bishop.
A locked centre with holes favours the knight.
Your only bishop moves on light squares. Where should your pawns stand?
tap to reveal
On dark squares. Then they don't block your bishop, and between them the pawns and the bishop control both colours. Pawns on the bishop's colour do the bishop's job twice and leave the other colour full of holes.
Principle 04 · fit the army

Open files and the 7th rank

Heavy artillery needs roads.

Artillery is the strongest unit in an army, and it's useless in a forest. It needs roads to reach the front.

Rooks are the artillery of chess: powerful, long-range, unable to jump. Behind closed pawns they are spectators. An open file (no pawns on it) is a road into the enemy camp, and the destination is the 7th rank, where the enemy pawns started. There they can't protect each other, and the king is trapped behind them.

First principle

A rook's power is the length of its open lines. Take the open file first, double on it so the opponent can't contest it, then use it as a road to the 7th rank. Two rooks on the 7th ("pigs on the seventh") usually decide the game.

A file with no pawns, or with only an enemy pawn on it (a half-open file).
An entry square on the file that the enemy can't cover.
Enemy pawns still sitting on their starting rank.
Why is the 7th rank so special?
tap to reveal
The enemy pawns that haven't moved are on it, and a rook attacks them from the side, where no pawn can defend another. And the enemy king on the back rank is cut off: it can't step forward. One rook there attacks pawns and imprisons the king at the same time.
Principle 05 · fit the army

Improve your worst piece

A team plays as well as its weakest player.

A football team with ten brilliant players and one who stands in the wrong place loses the ball through that one player, every time. A smart coach doesn't add another star. He fixes the weak link.

When there's nothing to calculate (no threats, no tactics), most players drift. Strong players ask one question: which of my pieces is doing the least? Then they give it a job, even if getting there takes four moves.

First principle

Your position is a chain, and it breaks at its weakest link. In quiet positions, time is cheap. The opponent can't punish slow moves through a locked centre, so spend that time on long manoeuvres that bring a sleeping piece into the game. The king counts too, especially once the queens are gone.

A piece with no targets and no good squares.
A piece blocked behind its own pawns.
A king still hiding when the queens are off the board.
Which piece is often the worst in the middlegame and the best in the endgame?
tap to reveal
The king. While queens are on the board it must hide. Once they're gone it becomes a strong fighting piece that can walk to the centre. Fischer's king walk against Petrosian is in the case files.
Principle 06 · out-think the plan

Prophylaxis

The best goalkeeper never has to dive.

Average goalkeepers make spectacular saves. Great goalkeepers are standing in the right spot before the shot, so the striker doesn't even shoot. Their saves look boring because they happened earlier.

Prophylaxis means "preventive medicine". Before you pursue your own plan, ask what your opponent wants to do next, and quietly make it impossible. An opponent without a plan slowly runs out of good moves.

First principle

Every move changes what's possible for both players, so a move that kills the opponent's best idea is often worth more than one that improves your own. Nimzowitsch taught a sequence: restrain the enemy pawn break, blockade it, then destroy.

The opponent has one freeing pawn break. Clamp it.
There's an enemy piece manoeuvre you can see coming. Take its target square.
Your plan would win easily if only his counterplay didn't exist.
What's the one question to ask before every move in a quiet position?
tap to reveal
"If it were his move, what would he play?" If the answer is strong, deal with it first. If it's harmless, go ahead with your own plan. It takes five seconds and it's the habit that separates strong players from tactical ones.
Principle 07 · out-think the plan

The principle of two weaknesses

One fire can be fought. Fires on two floors can't.

A fire crew can put out a fire on the ground floor. But when a second fire starts on the tenth floor, the crew has to choose, and the stairs between the floors take time.

A single weakness in chess can almost always be defended: the defender just parks pieces next to it. So the attacker creates a second weakness far away. The defender's pieces have to run back and forth across the board, and at some point they don't arrive in time.

First principle

Defence is about moving defenders, and moving takes time. Fix the first weakness so it ties down enemy pieces, then open a second front on the other wing. Soviet coaches attached a companion rule: don't hurry. Improve everything before you open the second front, because a tied-down opponent can't punish you for being slow.

Enemy pieces glued to defending one target.
A pawn break available on the other wing.
A locked centre that slows the defender's pieces when they try to switch sides.
Why should the second weakness be far away from the first?
tap to reveal
Because the defender's problem is distance. Two weaknesses next to each other can be guarded by the same pieces. Two weaknesses on opposite wings force the defender to split his army or keep running, and a locked centre makes the run even longer.

You now know all seven. Positional chess is about seeing them without anyone pointing.
